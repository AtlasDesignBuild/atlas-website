Kitchen design ideas that hold up over time

By Rachel Foy · Kitchen remodeling · Published July 22, 2026

Most kitchen design content online is built around what is trending this year. We think that is the wrong question to start with. A kitchen remodel is a five to seven figure investment that a family lives with for fifteen to twenty years, and the kitchens that feel most successful years later are rarely the ones that chased a trend.

They are the ones built around good layout, honest materials and choices made for how the household actually lives. This guide pulls from the design decisions we return to on project after project in Bucks County, Montgomery County and the surrounding Philadelphia suburbs. It is meant to help you think through your own kitchen before you get deep into product selections.

Start with how you actually use the kitchen

Before any layout or material conversation, we ask clients how they actually live in the space. Does one person cook while everyone else gathers around the island? Do kids do homework at the counter while dinner gets made? Is this a kitchen that hosts twelve people twice a year or two people every night? The answers change the design more than any style preference does.

A kitchen designed around real habits reads as effortless years later. A kitchen designed around a magazine photo often gets quietly worked around once the family moves in. If you are planning a kitchen remodel, that conversation about how you live is where we start on every project.

Layout ideas that hold up

Zones instead of a single triangle. The classic cook, clean and prep triangle still matters, but most working kitchens now function better as separate zones — a cooking zone, a prep zone, a cleanup zone and a landing zone — that can each host more than one person at a time.

Islands sized for real use. An island earns its footprint when it handles seating, prep and storage at once, not when it is sized purely as a visual centerpiece. In an older Doylestown home with a smaller original footprint, a smaller well planned island beats an oversized one that chokes the walkways.

Landing space by the range and refrigerator. Counter space on at least one side of the range and beside the refrigerator door swing is one of the most requested changes we make in kitchens that were laid out decades ago.

Aisles wide enough for two people. We plan working aisles at 42 to 48 inches so two people can work at once without the kitchen feeling tight.

Sightlines into connected living space. If the kitchen opens to a living or dining area, the layout should let whoever is cooking stay part of the conversation.

Cabinetry choices that age well

Simple door profiles — shaker and flat panel — have stayed current across every style shift of the last two decades, while ornate raised panel doors read as dated the moment a new style takes hold. Full overlay construction and quality hardware do more for how a kitchen feels ten years in than a trend driven cabinet color.

We also favor a mix of closed storage with a modest amount of open shelving over shelving that is open throughout. Fully open shelving photographs well and is hard to live with day to day. A furniture style element — a hutch look on one run of cabinetry, or furniture legs on an island — adds warmth without pinning the kitchen to a specific era.

Countertops and backsplash

Natural stone and quality quartz outlast whichever specific slab pattern is popular in a given year. Full height backsplash — stone or tile running from counter to cabinet or ceiling — has become the more current expectation over the standard eighteen inch backsplash, though both are still reasonable choices depending on budget and kitchen style.

One thing we consistently steer clients away from is pairing a highly patterned counter with a highly patterned backsplash. Let one surface carry the pattern and keep the other quiet.

Lighting layers, not just fixtures

A kitchen that feels right in any era usually has three lighting layers working together: ambient light from recessed fixtures, task light from under cabinet lighting at the work surfaces, and accent light from pendants or sconces. Pendant fixtures are also one of the lowest risk places to bring in a trend, since they are inexpensive to swap later if a style falls out of favor. Dimmers on every layer are worth the small added cost.

Color palettes that do not date

Cabinetry in a neutral tone — white, off white, warm wood, or a deep green or navy used as an accent — holds up better than a highly saturated color applied to every cabinet in the room. If you want to bring in a trend color, we usually suggest introducing it through the island only, a backsplash accent, or hardware, rather than the full kitchen.

In older homes throughout Bucks County, where there is often a lot of natural wood trim, plaster texture and older glass, a warm white reads better against those materials than a stark, cool white.

Where to spend and where it matters less

Spend on cabinetry construction, countertops and the appliances used daily — these are the elements that are expensive and disruptive to change later. Save on cabinet hardware, wall color and decorative elements — these are inexpensive to update in a few years if your taste shifts.

Trend versus timeless

Trend-driven choice What tends to hold up
All open shelving throughout A mix of open and closed storage
One bold accent color on every cabinet Neutral base with color in one or two features
Matte black hardware exclusively Warm metal tones mixed with black
A busy pattern on both counter and backsplash Pattern on one surface, quiet on the other
Fully open concept with no defined zones Defined work zones within an open layout

Frequently asked questions

Does a kitchen remodel need to match my home's architecture?

Not always, but it should feel intentional either way. In an older Bucks County home we often work with the existing character — original trim, window proportions, ceiling height — rather than against it. A kitchen that ignores the rest of the house tends to feel disconnected once the work is finished.

How many colors should be in a kitchen?

We generally aim for two to three main tones — a cabinetry color, a counter or backsplash tone, and one accent — rather than a wider palette that competes with itself.

Is open shelving practical for daily use?

For most households, a small amount works better than a lot. Open shelving needs to stay tidy and dust free, which is a real maintenance commitment in a kitchen that gets used daily.

What is the most common regret we hear from clients?

Not enough landing space near the range and refrigerator, and not enough deep storage for the items used only occasionally.

Should I design for how I live now or for resale value?

Both matter, but they are not the same question. We cover this in more depth in our guide to kitchen remodel ROI and home value. In general, layout quality and cabinetry construction hold value regardless of trend, which is also why they are worth prioritizing for your own daily use.

How long does the design phase take before construction starts?

Design and selections typically take longer than most homeowners expect — often several weeks to a few months depending on the scope and how quickly decisions get made. We walk through a realistic timeline with every client during the discovery phase.
