When to start planning a spring remodel

To start a remodel in spring, begin planning the fall before. A bathroom or basement needs roughly 4 to 5 months of planning lead time, a kitchen roughly 5 to 6 months and a whole home renovation or addition 6 to 9 months, because design, material selection and permitting take longer than most people expect.

By Rachel Foy, Co-founder & Design · Planning · Published September 28, 2026

Reviewed by John J. Foy, Co-founder & Design

The homeowners who break ground on a spring remodel without a scramble are almost always the ones who started planning the fall before. Design, material selection and permitting all take real time, and that time does not compress just because a start date is approaching.

This guide works backward from a spring start to show when each stage of planning actually needs to begin, whether the project is a kitchen, a bathroom or something larger.

Why remodel timelines run longer than people expect

Construction itself is often the shortest phase of a remodel. Design development, material selection, structural or engineering review when needed, and permitting with the local township typically take longer than the build itself, and none of these steps can be rushed without risking a change order or a delay once construction starts. Starting the process in fall or early winter gives each of these stages the time it actually needs, so a spring start date holds.

Working backward from a spring start date

Rather than asking how long a remodel takes, the more useful question is when planning needs to begin for construction to start on a specific date. A kitchen or bathroom remodel with a spring start generally needs design and material decisions finalized by late winter, with permitting submitted well ahead of that so approval is in hand before crews are scheduled. A larger project, a whole home renovation or an addition, needs that process to begin even earlier because of the additional engineering and permitting involved.

Planning lead time by project type

What happens in each phase before construction starts

  • Design development: This is where layout, materials and finishes get decided, and it is the phase most homeowners underestimate for how many decisions it actually involves.
  • Permitting: Once design is set, permitting begins, and timelines here vary by township and by project scope. A bathroom remodel with no structural changes moves faster through permitting than an addition does.
  • Material lead times: Ordering runs in parallel with permitting for custom cabinetry, certain tile and natural stone, and specialty fixtures, some of which take 8 to 12 weeks to arrive once ordered.

A sample fall-to-spring planning timeline

A kitchen remodel targeting a March start is a useful example.

  • October through December: Design and material selection.
  • December or January: Permitting submission, timed so approval is in hand before crews are scheduled.
  • As soon as design is final: Custom cabinetry and any long lead-time materials get ordered, so they arrive on schedule rather than holding up the start date once permits clear.

Frequently asked questions

Is it too late to start planning if I want a spring remodel and haven't started yet?

It depends on project type and scope. A bathroom remodel has more flexibility than a kitchen or an addition. Reach out and we can map a realistic timeline based on where things stand.

Why does permitting take so long?

Permitting timelines are set by the township, not by us, and vary based on project scope and that township's current volume. Projects involving structural, electrical or plumbing changes typically take longer to review than a cosmetic update.

Can I lock in pricing before design is finalized?

Pricing firms up as design and material selections are finalized, since both directly affect cost. We can provide a general range earlier in the process to help with budgeting.

Does starting early cost more than starting later?

No, planning timeline does not change project cost. Starting earlier mainly protects the target start date and gives more time to source materials without rushing a decision.

What if my material selections change after permitting is submitted?

Minor selections, like a tile or hardware choice, usually have no effect on an in-progress permit. A layout change after submission can require a resubmission, which is part of why finalizing design before permitting matters.

Do smaller projects need this much lead time?

A smaller cosmetic update, refreshing finishes without layout changes, generally moves faster through design and often does not require the same permitting review as a project with structural or plumbing changes.
